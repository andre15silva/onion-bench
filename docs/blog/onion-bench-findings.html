Onion-Bench Findings
onion-bench · benchmark report · October 2026

AI models find the joke, but not the lie

We gave two Claude models news from after their training cutoff. They identified satire easily. A small, believable change to a real article got past them most of the time.

What we tested

A large language model (LLM) learns from text up to a cutoff date. It does not know about events after that date. Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 have a cutoff in June 2026.

We collected news from July to October 2026. Each item is real or fake. We gave each item to each model and asked one question: “Is this news real or fake?” The model also gave a probability that the item is fake.

The models had no internet access and no tools. They could not do a search. They had only the text and their own knowledge. We asked each question five times, in five separate calls.

The test set

The test set has six groups. Three groups are fake and three groups are real. The items are in English and in European Portuguese.

To make the edited news, a third model (Claude Haiku 5.5) changed one fact in a real article. For example, “approved” became “refused”, or “dies” became “injured”. The change is in the headline and in the first lines. All other words stay the same. Each edited article keeps a link to its original article.

We used two versions of the test. In the full text version, the model sees the headline and the article. In the headline only version, the model sees only the headline. Viral claims have no article, so they are only in the headline-only version. Per language, each version has equal numbers of real and fake items.

Result 1: the models find satire easily

Both models called almost all satire “fake”. With the full text, the score was 99% for both models. With the headline only, the score was 94–95%. Ordinary real news was also safe. The models called it “fake” in only 1–5% of answers.

Result 2: a small edit gets past the models

Edited real news was the most difficult group. With the full text, Opus called it “fake” in 21% of answers. Sonnet called it “fake” in 10% of answers. With the headline only, both models were at 6–8%.

The models were also confident. Figure 2 shows the probability of “fake” for each item. The edited articles are near their real originals. They are far from the satire.

The text gives the models a small signal. For Opus, the edited article had a higher probability of “fake” than its original in 63% of pairs. For Sonnet, this was 60%. But the difference is small, so it almost never changes the answer.

Result 3: real news about new events can look fake

Some real news received the answer “fake”. In many of these items, the news is about an event after the cutoff that changes what the model knows. The model compares the news with its old knowledge and rejects the news.

This is a real risk. A model that checks facts against old knowledge can reject true news about a new event. Real but strange stories did not have this problem with the full text. The details in the article helped the models.

The two models make different errors

The two models have the same total score. We use the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), where 1 is perfect and 0 is chance. With the full text, both models have an MCC of 0.77. With the headline only, Opus has 0.70 and Sonnet has 0.69. The difference is not statistically significant.

But the errors are different. Opus answers “fake” more often. It catches more false claims, but it also rejects more true claims.

How we kept the test fair

  • The model sees only the headline and the article text. The outlet name, the URL, the author and the date stay in the dataset. They never go into the prompt.
  • Some articles name their own outlet, for example “told the BBC”. We did not change the text. We removed these items from the test.
  • The models had no tools. Each call was a separate session with no memory of other calls.
  • We read the exact request that goes to the model. We found two problems and fixed them before the final run. The name of the work folder (“onion-bench”) was visible to the model. Also, 68 Onion items had a list of other Onion headlines instead of article text.
  • We moved the style of The Onion to real articles, and we removed it from Onion articles. The style is datelines and Title Case headlines. The answers changed very little, so the models use the content, not the style.
  • We gave the models only the headlines of Onion articles and asked for the first sentence. They could not write the real sentence, so the articles were not in their training data.
  • We report MCC, recall, specificity and AUROC, with 95% confidence intervals from a bootstrap over items. The five answers to one item are almost always the same (Fleiss κ 0.95–0.98).

Limits

  • Claude Haiku 5.5 made the edited news. It is from the same model family as the models under test.
  • Some fact-check labels are about an image, not about the text. Observador rated a claim about a Himalayan flood “Errado” (wrong) because the images came from a different event. The text alone is true. Both models called it “real”.
  • With the full text, Portuguese satire has only 7 items. Some of them are opinion columns, not news. The 71 Inimigo Público headlines are only in the headline-only test.
  • Small groups have wide confidence intervals. For example, the “real but strange” group has 27 items.

What this means for AI safety

Satire is not a good test for fact checking. The models find absurd text easily, but a believable false fact gets past them. Tests for AI safety must include believable fakes, not only jokes.

The models must also show more uncertainty about events after their cutoff. They gave edited news a low probability of “fake”, but they had no information to support that confidence. A model that does not know must say that it does not know.

Method summary. 1,891 items collected from July to October 2026. Each model received each item five times, with the Claude Code command line (claude -p) and no tools. Full text: 724 items. Headline only: 1,206 items. Real and fake items are balanced per language. The code is in the onion-bench repository.
